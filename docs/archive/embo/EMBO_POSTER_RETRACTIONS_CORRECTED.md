## **CATEGORY 1: P-HACKING & STATISTICAL MANIPULATION**

### **Case 1.1: Brian Wansink (Cornell Food & Brand Lab)**

**Status:** 17+ papers retracted
**Journals:** JAMA, JAMA Pediatrics, Appetite, PLOS ONE
**Timeline:** 2007-2018 (retractions 2017-2018)

**Statistical Errors:**
- P-hacking via data mining ("dig into the data and get me a significant result")
- Impossible p-value patterns
- Misreported sample sizes
- Selective outcome reporting
- Post-hoc hypothesis testing (HARKing)

**Evidence:**
- Internal emails explicitly requesting p-hacking
- Statistical impossibilities identified by peer review
- Cornell investigation confirmed misconduct

**Impact:**
- 17+ papers retracted across top journals
- Resignation from Cornell University
- Major blow to nutrition science credibility
- Thousands of citations to retracted work

**Guardian Could Have Prevented:**
✓ Detected multiple testing without correction
✓ Flagged suspiciously perfect p-values (clustering near 0.05)
✓ Identified outcome switching patterns
✓ Required power analysis documentation

**Citations:**
- Lee, C. J., et al. (2018). "Statement on p-hacking." *Journal of Communication*, 68(3), 423-424.
- Van Noorden, R. (2018). "Publishers get tough on fake peer review." *Nature*.
- Retraction Watch: https://retractionwatch.com/the-retraction-watch-leaderboard/

### **Case 1.3: Psychology Replication Crisis (Large-Scale Study)**

**Study:** Open Science Collaboration (2015)
**Journal:** *Science*

**Findings:**
- Only **36%** of 100 key psychology experiments replicated successfully
- Original studies: 97% reported significant results
- Replication studies: 36% achieved significance

**Statistical Problems Identified:**
- Underpowered studies (median power = 35%)
- P-hacking (selective reporting of outcomes)
- HARKing (Hypothesizing After Results Known)
- Publication bias (file drawer problem)

**Guardian Could Have Prevented:**
✓ Required adequate power analysis (>80%)
✓ Blocked tests with violated assumptions
✓ Flagged multiple comparisons without correction

**Citation:**
- Open Science Collaboration. (2015). "Estimating the reproducibility of psychological science." *Science*, 349(6251), aac4716.

**Guardian Could Have Prevented:**
✓ Blocked population-level inference from n=12
✓ Flagged absence of control group
✓ Required power analysis for causal claims
✓ Identified selection bias in case recruitment

**Citations:**

## **CATEGORY 5: ECONOMIC/POLICY IMPACT**

### **Case 5.1: Reinhart-Rogoff Economic Growth & Debt Study**

**Journal:** *American Economic Review Papers & Proceedings*
**Timeline:** 2010 (error discovered 2013)

**Statistical Errors:**
- **Excel spreadsheet error**: Excluded rows 30-44 from mean calculation
- Selective country exclusion
- Unconventional weighting scheme
- Failure to share data/code for verification

**Impact:**
- Influenced **austerity policies** in US, UK, EU (2010-2013)
- Cited by policymakers to justify spending cuts
- **Billions in economic policy decisions** based on flawed calculation
- Congressional testimony based on erroneous statistics

**Corrected Analysis:**
- Original claim: >90% debt → -0.1% GDP growth
- Corrected result: >90% debt → +2.2% GDP growth
- **Complete reversal of policy implication**

**Guardian Could Have Prevented:**
✓ Required code/data sharing for reproducibility
✓ Automated calculation verification
✓ Flagged selective data exclusion
✓ Required sensitivity analysis

**Citations:**
- Herndon, T., Ash, M., & Pollin, R. (2014). "Does high public debt consistently stifle economic growth? A critique of Reinhart and Rogoff." *Cambridge Journal of Economics*, 38(2), 257-279.
- Reinhart, C. M., & Rogoff, K. S. (2010). "Growth in a Time of Debt." *American Economic Review*, 100(2), 573-578.
